05:30–06:00 Wave-coupled effects and modelling of the Marginal Ice Zone, Arctic and Antarctica Alexander Babanin 16:30–17:00 (AEDT, Australia)
Read abstract

By its nature, wave-ice-ocean interactions are a strongly coupled problem. Ice attenuates the surface waves, but waves can also break the ice in which case the dissipation drops by two orders of magnitude. Once the ice is broken, albedo is decreased (and dark ocean is exposed), so ice can melt more rapidly (and wave fetches and wave height will increase), or (depending on the air temperature) the ocean can refreeze and damp the waves. Moreover, an additional ocean mixing due to the presence of waves changes the sea surface temperature and hence the ice thickness. The most critical and the least explored process in these interactions is the ice breakup after which the nature of interactions changes abruptly. In the presentation, we discuss sea ice break-up parameterisation and demonstrate the existence of an observational threshold separating breaking and non-breaking cases. This threshold is based on information from two recent field campaigns (in the Arctic and Antarctica), supplemented with existing observations of sea ice break-up. The data used cover a wide range of scales, from laboratory-grown sea ice to polar field observations. We show that both field and laboratory observations tend to converge to a single quantitative threshold at which the wave-induced sea ice breakup takes place. Such threshold opens a promising avenue for robust parametrization in operational forecasting models. We introduced two independent wave-coupled effects in a high-resolution coupled ice-ocean model. The first parameterisation simulates the wave-induced sea ice break-up (the above-mentioned experimental threshold causes sudden albedo change). Our results show that the break-up leads to an increase in the sea ice concentration and thickness during the ice growth season, but accelerates the sea ice melt in summer. The second parameterisation presents influence of the wave mixing on the sea ice. We show that it can decelerate the sea ice formation in winter and the sea ice melt in summer by exchanging the heat fluxes between the surface and subsurface layers. In the Arctic, this effect is small, but in the Antarctic Ocean the wave induced mixing assists with decreasing the known ice-thickness summer bias in climate models. Other wave-ice problems and their experimental and modelling research will be outlined. These include different mechanisms of wave attenuation and dissipation in the ice, implementation of relevant physics in WW3 and WW3-ROMS/MOM6 coupled systems.

06:00–06:30 Reducing sea-ice thickness in the Antarctic MIZ due to surface wave–sea ice interaction: A proof-of-concept study Phil Reid 17:00–17:30 (AEDT, Australia)
Read abstract

The two main fluxes associated with melting sea ice within models are basal and lateral heat fluxes. Massom et al. (2026) identify an additional process of surface ablation driven by ocean wave-sea ice interaction within the MIZ that may be a missing factor, particularly during the annual sea ice retreat phase. The interaction comprises three sets of processes involving "wave flooding", "wave pulverisation" and "wave greening", with these processes leading to a change in sea ice albedo and enhanced melting through summer. It is suggested that implementing these processes may aid in improving model simulation of the observed mean annual cycle of Antarctic sea ice, sea ice forecasting and climate model outlooks. Here we explore a proof-of-concept study where we post-process coupled model forecasts and synthetically melt some of the sea ice based on the findings from this paper. Initial results, presented here, show promise.

06:30–07:00 Wave Loads on an Oscillating Circular Cylinder in a Shear-Layer Fluid under Ice Cover Izolda V. Sturova 13:30–14:00 (UTC+7, Russia)
Read abstract

The problem of oscillations of submerged bodies in a fluid beneath a free surface has been studied fairly well when the fluid in the undisturbed state is at rest or has a horizontal velocity that is constant with depth. Review of similar studies for fluid beneath an ice cover is contained in [1]. However, in real sea conditions, the vertical distribution of current velocity in some cases shows significant changes with the depth. This fact indicates that the study of flexural- gravity waves should be also carried out within the framework of such theoretical models that take into account the vertical structure of currents. In the present study, the linear 2D problem of steady-state oscillations of a circular cylinder is solved. An inviscid, homogeneous and incompressible fluid of finite depth consists of two layers. We consider one of the simplest examples of shear flow, in which the near-surface layer has a linear velocity shear, and the lower layer is at rest in the unperturbed state. The fluid is covered by an ice cover floating with the fluid, which is modeled by a thin elastic plate of constant thickness and density. The lower fluid layer contains a circular cylinder oscillating at a given frequency. The presence of the shear layer leads to the appearance of blocking points in the dispersion curves, at which the group velocity of the developing flexural-gravity waves vanishes. The linear approximation becomes unsuitable in the vicinity of these points. Using multipole expansions, the dependences of the added mass and damping coefficients on the characteristics of the ice cover and shear flow, the oscillation frequency, and the cylinder submergence depth are investigated. It is found that the diagonal added mass coefficients become negative at small distances of the cylinder from the interface and at low oscillation frequencies. The presented results were recently published in [2].

07:00–07:30 Flexural-gravity waves in a nonuniform ice system with a polynya induced by oscillations of a submerged body Tatyana Sibiryakova 14:00–14:30 (UTC+7, Russia)
Read abstract

A two-dimensional problem is considered concerning the propagation and interaction of dispersive waves in the system “semi-infinite free surface – finite ice cover – polynya – semi-infinite ice cover.” Gravity waves are excited by an oscillating submerged body in the open-water regions, while flexural-gravity waves are generated beneath the ice covers. The fluid is ideal, incompressible, and of finite depth H; the flow is potential. The ice-covered regions are modeled as thin elastic plates. The edges of the finite plate and the edge of the semi-infinite ice cover are free. The submerged body is modeled as a two-dimensional point dipole with a harmonically time-varying strength, located at an arbitrary point in the fluid domain. In an unbounded fluid, this model corresponds to small translational oscillations of a circular cylinder. Horizontal, vertical, and arbitrary inclined directions of dipole oscillations are considered. The free-surface elevations, ice-plate deflections, and fluid velocity potential are sought as steady harmonic oscillations at a given frequency. The velocity potential satisfies the Poisson equation with a dipole source. At the free edges of the ice plates, the bending moment and shear force are set to zero. At the vertical boundaries between adjacent regions, the velocity potential and its horizontal derivative are continuous. The problem is solved using the method of vertical modes. In the free-surface regions, vertical modes corresponding to gravity waves in a fluid of finite depth are used, while beneath the ice plates, modes corresponding to flexural-gravity waves are employed. In the finite regions corresponding to the polynya and the finite ice plate, waves propagating in both horizontal directions are taken into account. This approach describes wave reflection and transmission at the free edges of the ice plates and at the boundaries between different regions of the upper surface. The unknown coefficients in the modal expansions are determined from the matching conditions at the vertical boundaries. The solution for an arbitrary direction of dipole oscillations is constructed as a linear combination of the solutions for horizontally and vertically oriented dipoles. The influence of the oscillation frequency of the submerged body, the fluid depth, and the direction of dipole oscillations on the hydroelastic response of the nonuniform ice system is investigated. The amplitudes of the excited gravity and flexural-gravity waves, the spatial distributions of ice-plate deflections and bending strains, and the wave-interaction features caused by the finite ice plate and the polynya are analyzed.

07:30–08:00 Earlier Retreat and Later Return Contribute Almost Equally to a Longer Arctic Low Ice Season M. Ghathfan Kinandityo 14:30–15:00 (WIB, Indonesia)
Read abstract

The duration of low Arctic sea-ice extent depends on when ice retreats and when it returns. We quantified those contributions using daily Northern Hemisphere extent from the NSIDC Sea Ice Index, Version 4, for 1989–2025. For each year, retreat was the first day of seven consecutive days below 8 million km\\(^2\\). Return was the first day of seven consecutive days at or above that threshold after the annual minimum. Their difference defined a low-extent season on a common 365-day calendar. Annual linear trends were estimated with heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent confidence intervals using three annual lags. Thresholds of 9 and 10 million km\\(^2\\) provided sensitivity checks. Across 37 years, retreat advanced by 8.65 days per decade and return was delayed by 8.69 days per decade. The low-extent season lengthened by 17.34 days per decade (95% confidence interval 13.93–20.75). Later return accounted for 50.1% of that trend. Mean duration increased from 62.1 days in 1989–1998 to 109.0 days in 2016–2025. A Theil–Sen estimate gave a similar duration trend of 17.62 days per decade. Duration also increased at the higher thresholds, by 15.88 and 14.49 days per decade. The longer low-extent season reflects nearly equal contributions from earlier retreat and later return at the primary threshold. Reporting both crossings makes this seasonal change visible in a way that an annual minimum alone cannot. These are hemispheric, threshold-dependent indicators. They should not be interpreted as local ice-free dates or shipping-access windows, and the trend decomposition does not attribute the changes to particular physical drivers.

Keywords: Arctic sea ice; seasonal timing; threshold crossing; Sea Ice Index

15:30–16:00 Modelling Wave-Ice Interactions in Three-Dimensions in the Beaufort Sea Marginal Ice Zone Will Perrie 11:30–12:00 (AST, Canada)
Read abstract

We compare five wave–ice model parameterizations implemented in WAVEWATCH III for wave evolution in the Beaufort Sea marginal ice zone (MIZ), using controlled rectangular-ocean experiments and observations from the 2015 Sea State Boundary Layer Experiment. The model set includes viscous-layer, empirical attenuation, and three-dimensional scattering paramterizations, including a scattering model with an additional dissipative loss term. The rectangular-ocean simulations isolate the intrinsic responses of these parameterizations to prescribed ice concentration, floe size, floe thickness and wind direction. Two field cases then test these responses under realistic, evolving winds, sea ice and drifting-buoy sampling: a large-wave, low-ice, near-collinear event and a cross-winds event with more persistent MIZ ice. Comparisons use significant wave height, one-dimensional spectra, directional spread and apparent slantwise attenuation between buoy pairs. In the cross-winds case, waves attenuate and turn towards the normal to the ice edge. Directional-spread observations provide a strong constraint: scattering-dominated parameterizations generally over-broaden spectra in ice, whereas primarily dissipative parameterizations show little directional response. The results support a multiprocess, dissipation-dominated description of MIZ wave evolution, in which modest directional scattering is accompanied by effective dissipative losses. No single parameterization is uniformly superior because model–data differences also reflect uncertainty in winds, ice conditions and buoy trajectories.

16:00–16:30 Wave-induced erosion of ice cliffs: Melt rate due to boundary layer currents Nimish Pujara 08:00–08:30 (PST, Canada)
Read abstract

Wave erosion is one of the dominant deterioration mechanisms for ocean icebergs, while also contributing to the ablation of ice shelves and marine-terminating glacier fronts. Oscillatory flow due to wave action efficiently erodes notches into the ice, which over time results in calving of both the overhang and the submerged foot—consequences that make wave erosion especially effective at rapidly breaking down large bodies of ice. Despite being widely considered a key driver of ice melting and breakup, this mechanism, and the impact of oscillatory flow in general, is only now receiving new attention since a handful of experiments and accompanying theory introduced the existing parameterizations decades ago. The previous parameterizations model the melt rates assuming that momentum transfer and heat transfer are correlated (i.e. a Reynolds analogy), but fundamentally rely on steady (or quasi-steady) flow assumptions. Here, we present a new theoretical framework for the problem with accompanying laboratory experiments. It is well-known that wave-induced oscillatory flow adjacent to walls produces a mean current known as boundary layer streaming. We calculate the form and magnitude of this current for waves reflecting from a vertical wall via a multi-timescale analysis and then compute the wave-averaged temperature distribution as a balance between vertical advection by the boundary layer streaming current and horizontal diffusion via a similarity solution. The melt rate is then computed using the Stefan condition for this wave-averaged temperature distribution. Our lab data provides good support for this new model. We measure a streaming current, with properties consistent with the theory, in laboratory experiments of wave reflection at a vertical wall. We also measure wave erosion of an ice wall with melt rates that are consistent with the model. Based on the combined theory and laboratory data, we propose that wave erosion of ice cliffs, and the interaction of oscillatory flow with ice in general, could be better modelled by explicitly considering the unsteady nature of oscillatory flow rather than relying on quasi-steady assumptions.

16:30–17:00 Satellite Sea Ice Concentration Observation Uncertainty Is State-Dependent and Exceeds Prescribed Values in Data Assimilation Joseph Rotondo 08:30–09:00 (PST, USA)
Read abstract

Earth system data assimilation systems commonly assimilate satellite retrievals of sea ice concentration (SIC). How much weight each retrieval receives relative to the model is set by its observation uncertainty, which is poorly constrained and is almost always prescribed as a constant, typically 0.10 to 0.15. We instead measure this uncertainty from the assimilation system itself. An iterated Desroziers diagnostic, computed in a bounded framework that keeps SIC between 0 and 1, gives the observation uncertainty for two passive microwave products and one optical product in both hemispheres. Bayes' rule then converts it into the uncertainty of a retrieval given the SIC it reports. That uncertainty is far from constant: it is near zero to 0.22 for a retrieval of open water (0) or complete cover (1) and 0.2 to 0.35 for a retrieval of one half. The exception is complete cover in the melt season, which the model rarely simulates and the method therefore likely cannot constrain properly. A similar curve emerges from three collocated satellite products, with no model involved, and from the uncertainty one product publishes with its retrievals. Most of the uncertainty is not instrument noise; its pattern is consistent with known passive microwave retrieval errors over thin and ponded ice. A constant error prescription therefore gives mid-range retrievals too much weight and often retrievals near 0 and 1 too little.

17:00–17:30 Improving Floe Welding and using Floe Health to Couple Rheology to the Sea Ice Floe Size Distribution Geraint W. Webb 09:00–09:30 (PST, USA)
Read abstract

Sea ice has traditionally been modelled as a continuum failing to capture the heterogeneity of individual pieces of ice called floes. More recently, the CICE6 sea ice model introduced a 12-bin discretized prognostic floe size distribution (FSD) that is evolved using six tendencies: new ice formation, lateral growth, lateral melt, welding (coagulation), wave fracture, and floe deformation (which is fragmentation driven by divergence in the ice velocity field). The welding of floes is modelled using the Smoluchowski coagulation equation, where a multiplicative coagulation kernel is used. The multiplicative kernel makes the floes weld biquadratically, with radius respect to their radii, and would cause the FSD (on an unbounded domain and in the absence of other tendencies) to produce an infinitely large floe in finite time - a phenomenon known as gelation. On a bounded domain with wave fracture enabled (as in CICE6) the resulting distribution concentrates floes in the smallest and largest bins, which leaves the intermediate bins sparsely populated. Within our work, we introduce a granular coagulation kernel that slows coagulation among the largest sea ice floes and allows more floes to populate the intermediate size bins. Building on this work, we then couple the FSD to floe health, which records how recently a floe was formed as the product of two smaller floes. Floe health is subsequently coupled to floe deformation and wave fracture, which increases fragmentation (as floes with a lower health fragment more). Health is also coupled to P*, within the Elastic–Viscous–Plastic rheology of CICE6, improving the simulation of shear and deformation. Thus, showcasing that including parameterizations of the FSD within a model can impact not only thermodynamics, but the dynamics of the sea ice itself.

17:30–18:00 Tsunami generation and propagation in ice-covered water Ikjae Lee 11:30–12:00 (CST, USA)
Read abstract

This study presents general analytical solutions for transient flexural-gravity waves propagating in an ice-covered sea under various wave generation mechanisms. Utilizing the joint Laplace-Fourier transform approach and the Kelvin-Voigt viscoelastic model, the long-term asymptotic behavior of these wave systems is comprehensively analyzed using the steepest descent method. The analysis reveals that long gravity waves are unaffected by ice viscosity and flexural rigidity; they are scaled solely by ice inertia, maintaining the exact decay rate of an inviscid, purely elastic ice plate. Furthermore, for seafloor motion, the associated transfer function acts as an effective low-pass filter that heavily suppresses short flexural waves independently of flexural rigidity. Key findings indicate distinct decay rates across different parts of the wave packets. Waves propagating at the minimum group velocity exhibit a slower decay rate of \\(O(t^{-1/3})\\), while generic locations decay at \\(O(t^{-1/2})\\). Crucially, at the leading wave front, the decay characteristics vary depending on the specific wave generation mechanism, exhibiting rates such as \\(O(t^{-2/3})\\) or \\(O(t^{-1})\\). This leads to highly asymmetric envelopes over long distances. For instance, disturbances from tilted seafloor motions or atmospheric pressure cause the wave packets to become blunter toward the trailing edge, which imply that the largest amplitude waves arrive later. The analysis also highlights that while most wave components maintain their wavelength over time when observed at their respective group velocities, the leading and trailing waves exhibit weak dispersiveness and spread gradually. Additionally, even minimal ice viscosity significantly alters waveforms by rapidly attenuating short-wavelength components compared to long gravity waves. Moreover, the study characterizes the propagation of internal stresses, which demonstrates that maximum bending and shear stresses decay much faster near the wave front than the actual free-surface displacement. This suggests that wave-induced ice fracturing from events like tsunamis is most likely to occur near the slower-moving trailing wave edges rather than the wave front. Finally, the study establishes the foundation for future three-dimensional extensions. Because classic stationary phase approximations is inapplicable near the leading edge, incorporating geometric spreading is necessary to estimate more realsitic tsunami wave packets in ice-covered waters.

07:30–08:00 June Conditions Outperform Calendar Trends in Retrospective Predictions of Antarctic September Sea Ice Extent Azzallea Ega Riesta Christsanda 14:30–15:00 (WIB, Indonesia)
Read abstract

A calendar trend can become a poor guide to Antarctic sea-ice extent when recent conditions diverge from its training history. We tested whether June extent supplied a more useful predictor of September conditions. Daily Southern Hemisphere extent from the NSIDC Sea Ice Index, Version 4, was averaged by calendar month for 1989–2025. Four models were compared in rolling-origin hindcasts for 2004–2025: the expanding historical September mean, a linear trend in year, a regression on June extent, and persistence of the June anomaly. Each target year was withheld from model fitting, leaving at least 15 prior years for training. Across 22 target years, root mean squared error was 0.449 million km² for June regression, compared with 0.699 for climatology and 0.714 for the calendar trend. June-anomaly persistence gave 0.554 million km\\(^2\\). The comparison changed over time. During 2004–2015, the calendar trend had a lower error than June regression (0.442 versus 0.507 million km\\(^2\\)). During 2016–2025, its error increased to 0.942 million km\\(^2\\), while June regression gave 0.368 and climatology 0.835 million km\\(^2\\). In that later period, the calendar trend overpredicted September extent by an average of 0.790 million km\\(^2\\); the June model's mean overprediction was 0.170 million km\\(^2\\). June regression beat climatology in seven of those ten years. A contemporaneous seasonal measurement improved these retrospective predictions, especially in the later evaluation period. The result supports retaining a simple June-based benchmark when testing more complex seasonal models. It does not establish an operational forecasting system: the evaluation contains only 22 targets, uses the current data release, and does not reproduce historical data availability or quantify forecast uncertainty.

Keywords: Antarctic Sea Ice; Seasonal Prediction; Rolling-Origin Evaluation; Hindcast

08:00–08:30 Influence of stepped bathymetry on the heave radiation of a floating rectangular buoy Rajdip Dey 13:30–14:00 (IST, India)
Read abstract

In the present study, the heave radiation of a floating rectangular buoy over a stepped seabed investigated assuming small amplitude water wave theory. The existence of a local seabed step causes an uneven bathymetry, which can significantly affect the hydrodynamic properties of a floating body and the wave fields radiated from it. Two cases are considered in the current investigation according to the position of the buoy with respect to the seabed step. In the first case, the buoy is placed symmetrically relative to the seabed step, while in the second case, the buoy is placed asymmetrically relative to the seabed step. The boundary value problem is then solved using the method of matched eigenfunction expansion (MEEM). Using the boundary conditions and interface matching conditions for each region of the fluid domain, the problem is solved in the form of a system of linear algebraic equations. A systematic parametric study is carried out to evaluate the influence of the water depth ratio, buoy draft, buoy width, and buoy position with respect to the seabed step on the hydrodynamic coefficients. The analytical solution compared for the case of a rectangular buoy undergoing heave motion over a flat seabed. The results show that the existence of a seabed step causes a significant hydrodynamic blockage effect, especially when the distance between the buoy and the seabed step becomes smaller. Such hydrodynamic blockage effect affects the wave pattern generated by the motion of the buoy, causing substantial changes in the added mass and radiation damping of the buoy. Moreover, the results have shown that an increase in the height of the step usually leads to an increase in the added mass and radiation damping, especially over the frequency range. Further, at the same time, the radiated wave field shows asymmetry for the left and right regions of the buoy. The effect of the location of the buoy in relation to the step has been evaluated. The buoy moved toward the shallow side, producing increased added mass and radiation damping compared to those located in symmetric or deeper positions. Nevertheless, local variations in bottom topography and the position of the buoy should be taken into account during the hydrodynamic evaluation of floating structures in areas where there are significant changes in water depth.

08:30–09:00 Scattering of Obliquely Incident Surface Gravity Waves by a Semi-Infinite Floating Elastic Plate in a Two-Layer Fluid Pawan Negi 14:00–14:30 (IST, India)
Read abstract

Due to numerous humanitarian activities in the marine environment, there has been a tremendous growth of knowledge in the hydroelastic analysis of the floating and submerged structures for ocean space utilisation in the last few decades. In the hydroelastic analysis of floating structures, surface gravity waves interact with flexible floating/submerged structures to generate flexural gravity waves. An analogous branch of study is the gravity wave interaction with the floating ice-sheet which is of interest in the cold region science and technology. In physical situations, ocean stratification may occur due to the difference in temperature, salinity and density in the marine environment. Moreover, at various locations, a layer of freshwater lies above the salty water in many channels, fjord and coastal areas. Thus, often the ocean water is modelled as a two-layer fluid having a free surface and an interface. Thus, it is pertinent to perform the hydroelastic analysis of the floating and/or submerged flexible structures in a two-layer fluid system in the ocean environment. Further, one of the noteworthy phenomena associated with flexural gravity waves is wave blocking, which reveals the existence of multiple propagating wave modes within a range of time-period for certain values of lateral compressive forces. Recent studies reveal that wave blocking occurs when the group velocity vanishes, whilst the phase velocity remains positive for two different values of the time period. In addition, for a particular value of compressive force and time-period, both the group velocity and its derivative vanishes, which is referred to as the threshold of blocking and the corresponding wavenumber is termed as the saddle point. In the present study, scattering of obliquely incident surface gravity waves by a semi-infinite floating elastic plate on a two-layer fluid within the framework of blocking dynamics is studied. The energy relations for the scattering problem will be derived in the case of single as well as multiple propagating modes. The amplitude of the individual waves and the associated scattering coefficients will be computed and analyzed in the vicinity of the blocking time period as well as the saddle point where root coalescence takes place. In addition, the role of various hydrodynamic and structural parameters on the scattering coefficients, critical angles and the deflections of the floating flexible structure will be analyzed.

09:00–09:30 Time-dependent wave propagation of flexural gravity wave over a viscoelastic ocean bed Ankita Chandra 14:30–15:00 (IST, India)
Read abstract

This paper investigates the propagation of flexural–gravity waves produced by initial disturbances in a finite-depth, incompressible ocean with a viscoelastic seabed. The ocean surface is covered by a thin floating ice sheet, which is modeled as an elastic plate using the Euler–Bernoulli beam theory. The effects of compressive forces acting within the ice sheet are also considered. The governing problem is formulated using linear water-wave theory, while the viscoelastic behavior of the seabed is described by Voigt’s model. The Laplace–Fourier transform method is used to derive integral expressions for the deflection of the ice sheet and the elevation of the ice–water interface. These integrals are then evaluated for large times and distances using the method of saddle point approximation to study the behavior of the wave profiles. The dispersion relation is also derived to understand how the ice sheet, ocean water, and viscoelastic seabed affect wave propagation. Numerical results are presented to examine the effects of important non-dimensional parameters, such as the flexural rigidity and compressive force of the ice sheet, the kinematic viscosity of the fluid, and the shear modulus of the seabed, on the ice-plate deflection and interface elevation. The results show that the combined effects of the floating ice sheet and the viscoelastic seabed influence the wave amplitude, propagation speed, and dispersion characteristics. They also demonstrate that the interaction between the ice sheet and the seabed plays an important role in the transient behavior of flexural–gravity waves. Finally, a limiting case is considered, and the agreement between the present results and previously published results confirms the validity of the analysis.

09:30–10:00 Effects of compressibility and in-plane compression on flexural–gravity and acoustic–gravity waves in a stratified ice-covered ocean Injamamul Haque Ahmed 15:00–15:30 (IST, India)
Read abstract

This study examines wave blocking in a compressible, two-layer stratified ocean beneath a thin elastic ice sheet under uniform in-plane compression, focusing on the contrasting behaviour of flexural–gravity waves (FGWs) and acoustic–gravity waves (AGWs). A unified hydroelastic model is formulated to account jointly for density stratification, fluid compressibility, and ice-plate elasticity. The analysis shows that wave blocking is confined entirely to the FGW branch and occurs at turning points of the dispersion relation, where the group velocity vanishes and energy can no longer be transported forward. These turning points define primary and secondary blocking regimes, beyond which wave energy becomes nearly stationary and, under sufficiently large compressive loads, may evolve toward instability. AGW modes, by contrast, exhibit monotonic dispersion curves with strictly positive group velocity and show no blocking in any of the regimes considered. The role of the dimensionless parameter \\(k_a\\), which measures the balance between gravitational restoring forces and ice-sheet inertia, is investigated to distinguish gravity-dominated from inertia-dominated regimes. While AGWs remain essentially unaffected in both limits, FGW dynamics are strongly controlled by the competition between compressive stress and plate rigidity. Importantly, fluid compressibility modifies the dispersion characteristics and shifts the critical buckling load of the ice sheet away from its classical incompressible value, thereby altering the onset of buckling in stratified ocean configurations. Time-domain simulations further illustrate the evolution of the wave field across regimes, revealing a clear progression for FGWs from regular propagation to blocked, near-stagnant states and ultimately to unstable responses, whereas AGWs continue to propagate without obstruction. These findings clarify how stratification, compressibility, and in-plane compression together govern energy transport and stability in ice-covered oceans, with implications for polar ocean dynamics and ice-sheet stability assessment.

10:00–10:30 Wave Scattering by an Infinite Step in the Presence of Thin and Thick Vertical Structures Sanjay Shakya 15:30–16:00 (IST, India)
Read abstract

This study investigates linear wave scattering by a composite configuration consisting of an infinite step, a thin vertical barrier, and a bottom-standing thick rigid structure in the finite-depth region. Two incidence scenarios are considered, with waves propagating from the finite-depth region toward the infinitely deep region and in the opposite direction. For each case, the boundary-value problem is formulated as three coupled first-kind integral equations for the unknown horizontal velocity distributions across the fluid passages. These equations are solved using a multi-term Galerkin method with weighted polynomial basis functions that capture the characteristic singularities at submerged corners and the edge of the thin barrier. The reflection and transmission coefficients are validated through energy conservation and limiting cases. Results show that increasing the barrier length generally enhances reflection and reduces transmission, while increasing the rigid-structure width intensifies resonance-induced oscillations. Variations in the structural dimensions and spacing modify the locations and amplitudes of the resonant peaks and troughs. The fluid-passage depth strongly influences wave transmission and the intensity of the oscillatory response, with its effect depending on the direction of incidence. The two incidence scenarios exhibit distinctly different scattering characteristics owing to the geometric and bathymetric asymmetry of the system.

Deep-sea mining risers in ultra-deep waters are subjected to depth-dependent shear currents and towing-induced hydrodynamic loads, both of which strongly influence structural behaviour. This study uses a validated finite element model to assess the effects of shear current profiles (linear, exponential, stepped), shear intensity (\\(\beta\\) = 0.05–0.95), towing conditions, and booster pump configurations. Results indicate that shear intensity is the dominant factor governing riser response. As \\(\beta\\) increases, lateral deflection and rotation rise significantly, while von Mises stress remains nearly constant (\\(\approx\\) 420–430 MPa). At low \\(\beta\\), the linear profile produces the largest deflections, up to 170% and 300% higher than those of the exponential and stepped profiles, respectively, while the differences become negligible (<2%) at higher \\(\beta\\), where the surface current magnitude governs the behaviour. Towing velocity is also critical: co-directional towing increases deformation by over an order of magnitude compared to no towing, whereas counter-directional towing reverses deformation with a similar magnitude. Booster pump stations introduce localised curvature and stress variations without significantly affecting global trends. Multi-booster setups yield higher deformation than a single bottom- mounted pump, though stress levels remain largely unchanged. Shear intensity and towing direction dominate riser behaviour. Accurate representation of the current profile is especially important at low-to-moderate shear, providing practical guidance for the design of multi- booster deep-sea mining riser systems.

Keywords: Deep-sea mining riser; Multi-booster configuration; Depth-varying shear current;

Towing-induced loading; Finite-element modelling.

12:30–13:00 Trapped modes in the water wave problem for partially submerged bodies and a floating ice plate Oleg V. Motygin 15:30–16:00 (MSK, Russia)
Read abstract

The two-dimensional linear problem of time-harmonic oscillations of an ideal incompressible fluid of infinite depth is considered in the presence of fixed partially submerged bodies and a finite ice plate floating on the free surface; the ice is modelled as a thin elastic plate. The velocity potential is harmonic in the fluid domain and satisfies the free-surface condition, the plate condition on the ice-covered part of the boundary, the free-edge conditions at the plate ends, the homogeneous Neumann condition on the wetted surfaces of the bodies, and the radiation condition at infinity. In the homogeneous case (zero Neumann data), we prove that any solution to the problem is localized: the potential and its gradient decay to zero at infinity. Here we construct examples of such localized solutions (trapped modes), which extend the known examples of non-uniqueness for the free-surface water-wave problem to the hydroelastic setting with a floating ice plate. The trapped mode is constructed by a semi-inverse procedure: a potential satisfying all the conditions except the Neumann one is prescribed, and the body contours are then chosen as streamlines of the conjugate stream function, on which the normal derivative of the potential vanishes by the Cauchy–Riemann equations. The potential combines a source (the Green function of the ice-covered water-wave problem) and an x-dipole placed at each of two points of the free surface. For a fixed position of the first singularity, the position of the second one and the coefficients in the combination of singularities are determined by eliminating waves propagating to infinity in both directions. The existence of streamlines enclosing the singularities is justified rigorously; families of such streamlines can be used to select the wetted contours of the bodies and thus to determine the fluid domain. Numerical results illustrate the streamline pattern, the geometry of the two bodies supporting the trapped mode, and the distributions of the bending moment and shear force on the ice plate.

13:00–13:30 Rethinking nonlinear wave-ice interaction: beyond additive sea ice damping in spectral wave models Rhys Ransome 13:00–13:30 (GMT, UK)
Read abstract

Ocean waves contribute to the break-up of sea ice, upper ocean mixing and other processes, highlighting the importance of wave forecasting in earth system models. Concurrently, sea ice damps the energy of ocean waves along their propagation. The spectral equations used to describe the evolution of wave energy in contemporary wave models assumes damping due to sea ice and non-linear wave energy exchange are independent. In this talk, we critically examine this assumption. Starting from the equations of weakly non-linear waves, we derive a governing equation that consistently couples damping and non-linear interactions. This does not lead to a change in total energy but compared to the standard additive damping approach there is a noticeable difference in the distribution of energy among the wave frequencies, particularly when damping is allowed to depend on wave frequency, as is characteristic of the interaction of waves with sea-ice. The formulation presented therefore provides a basis for future operational wave-ice modelling.

About MOSSI

Sea ice and ice sheets are important components of the Earth’s climate system, influencing planetary albedo, sea level, ocean circulation and interactions between the atmosphere, ocean and polar environment. Their dynamics involve fluid flow, elasticity, fracture, thermodynamics, wave propagation and processes acting across widely different spatial and temporal scales. Mathematical models are therefore essential for understanding these systems and predicting cryospheric change.

Aim of the Workshop

MOSSI brings together mathematicians, geophysicists, engineers and computational scientists to present new research, discuss challenging modelling problems and encourage interdisciplinary collaboration in sea-ice and ice-sheet mathematics.
